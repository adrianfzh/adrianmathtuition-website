## How to read this

- One scheme per skill. Each has the same five parts:
  what the question looks like · the levels · a short example at every level ·
  the one thing that lifts an answer to the next level · what the marker tags.
- The levels are **ours**. SEAB publishes the skills, not the level descriptors.
  These follow the pattern most school schemes share.
- A student sees a level **range** ("Level 2–3 of 4"). Never a mark.
- Every source and answer below is invented for this document.
- The tags the marker puts on each claim are the same in every scheme:
  **from the source** · **not supported** · **uses context** · **evaluates**.

## The rules that hold in every scheme

1. The level comes from the **best thing the answer does**, done properly once.
   Length does not move it.
2. A claim counts only when it is **supported**: a quote or a detail from the source,
   then a line saying how that detail shows the claim.
3. Copying the source is Level 1 everywhere.
4. A wrong reading of the source cannot be lifted by good structure.
5. Own knowledge counts only when it is used to **check or explain** the source.
   Own knowledge on its own is not a source-based answer.

## 1. Inference

**The question:** "What does Source A tell you about …?" · "What is the message of Source A?"

| Level | What the answer does |
|---|---|
| 1 | Copies or describes the source. Says what it shows, not what it means. |
| 2 | Makes a valid inference, but gives no evidence — or gives evidence that does not fit it. |
| 3 | Makes a valid inference, supports it from the source, and explains the link. |

A "message" question needs one more thing at Level 3: who the source is speaking to,
or what it wants them to think.

**Example source.** A resident's letter to a newspaper forum:
> "My block's lift has broken down four times this year. Each time, my neighbour in
> a wheelchair waited two days to leave her flat. The town council replied to my
> e-mail after three weeks."

**Question:** What does the source tell you about the town council?

- **Level 1:** "The lift broke down four times and the council replied after three weeks."
- **Level 2:** "The town council does not care about residents."
- **Level 3:** "The town council is slow to respond to residents' needs.
  It took 'three weeks' to reply to an e-mail about a lift that had failed
  'four times this year'. A problem that kept a wheelchair user at home for days
  was not treated as urgent."

**What lifts it**
- 1 → 2: stop repeating the source. Say what it *shows about* the town council.
- 2 → 3: quote the detail, then add one line on how it proves your point.

**Common slips:** an inference about the wrong thing (the lift, not the council);
an inference too big for the evidence ("the government has failed").

## 2. Comparison

**The question:** "How similar are Sources B and C?" · "How different are Sources B and C?"

| Level | What the answer does |
|---|---|
| 1 | Compares the wrong thing: where the sources come from, or their topic ("both are about buses"). Or describes each source in turn with no comparison. |
| 2 | One valid similarity **or** one valid difference in what the sources say, supported from **both** sources. |
| 3 | A similarity **and** a difference, each supported from both sources. |
| 4 | Level 3, and compares something deeper — the tone, the purpose, or whose side each source takes — supported. |

A comparison needs a **common point**: one thing both sources speak about.

**Example sources.**
- **B**, a bus company's press statement: "Fares will rise by 4 cents. The increase is
  needed to keep services reliable as fuel and wage costs go up."
- **C**, a commuter's online post: "Another fare rise, and my bus still comes every
  20 minutes. We pay more each year and get nothing back."

**Question:** How different are Sources B and C?

- **Level 1:** "Source B is by a company and Source C is by a commuter."
- **Level 2:** "They differ on whether the fare rise is fair. B says it is 'needed to
  keep services reliable'. C says commuters 'get nothing back'."
- **Level 3:** the Level 2 answer, and: "They are similar in agreeing that fares are
  going up. B says fares 'will rise by 4 cents' and C calls it 'another fare rise'."
- **Level 4:** Level 3, and: "They differ in purpose. B wants commuters to accept the
  rise, so it gives reasons. C wants other commuters to feel the rise is unfair,
  so it points to the long wait."

**What lifts it**
- 1 → 2: name the common point first, then quote both sources on it.
- 2 → 3: give the other side — a similarity if you gave a difference.
- 3 → 4: ask why each source was written, and compare that.

**Common slips:** quoting only one source; a "difference" that is two unrelated facts.

## 3. Reliability

**The question:** "How reliable is Source D as evidence about …?" · "Can you trust Source D?"
· "Does Source E prove Source D wrong?"

| Level | What the answer does |
|---|---|
| 1 | Judges by the type of source alone ("it is a newspaper, so it is reliable"), or simply accepts what it says. |
| 2 | Judges by the content alone: explains what the source says and calls it reliable, or points to loaded words with no check. |
| 3 | **Checks** the source: cross-refers to another source in the set, or to own knowledge, that agrees or disagrees. Says what the check shows. |
| 4 | Level 3, and weighs the **purpose**: who made it, for whom, and what they wanted — and how that affects trust. |

**Example sources.**
- **D**, a property developer's brochure: "Residents nearby fully support the new
  mall. It will bring jobs and convenience to everyone."
- **E**, a survey by a residents' committee: "62% of households said they are worried
  about traffic and noise from the mall."

**Question:** How reliable is Source D about residents' views of the mall?

- **Level 1:** "It is reliable because the developer knows about the mall."
- **Level 2:** "It is reliable because it says residents 'fully support' the mall and
  it will bring jobs."
- **Level 3:** "It is not reliable. D claims residents 'fully support' the mall, but
  Source E's survey found '62% of households' are worried about traffic and noise.
  The residents' own answers contradict D."
- **Level 4:** Level 3, and: "D is a brochure from the developer, who gains if the
  mall is approved. It is written to make buyers and planners think there is no
  opposition, so it leaves the worries out."

**What lifts it**
- 1 → 2: use what the source says, not what kind of source it is.
- 2 → 3: find another source that agrees or disagrees, and quote it.
- 3 → 4: say who made it, who it is for, and what they want from that reader.

**Common slips:** "biased" with no explanation; a cross-reference that only says
"Source E disagrees" without the quote; calling a source unreliable just because it
is one-sided.

## 4. Usefulness

**The question:** "How useful is Source F in helping you understand …?"

| Level | What the answer does |
|---|---|
| 1 | Judges by the type of source or the topic ("it is useful because it is about housing"). |
| 2 | Useful for what it **tells** us: an inference about the issue, supported. |
| 3 | Useful **and** limited: what it tells us, and what it leaves out that the question needs. |
| 4 | Tests whether it can be trusted — by a cross-reference or by its purpose — and uses that to judge how useful it is. |

Usefulness is always "useful **for what**". The answer must stay on the issue in the question.

**Example source.**
- **F**, a ministry poster: "Three in four seniors who joined our active-ageing
  centres say they feel less lonely."
- A second source in the set, a social worker's interview: "The seniors who most need
  company are the ones who never come to the centres."

**Question:** How useful is Source F in understanding whether loneliness among seniors is being solved?

- **Level 1:** "It is useful because it is from the ministry and is about seniors."
- **Level 2:** "It is useful. It shows the centres help those who join: 'three in four'
  say they 'feel less lonely'."
- **Level 3:** Level 2, and: "It is limited. It counts only seniors 'who joined'.
  It says nothing about seniors who stay at home."
- **Level 4:** Level 3, and: "The social worker says the seniors who most need company
  'never come to the centres'. So F's figure is true but covers the easier group.
  F is also a poster meant to bring more seniors in, so it shows only the good result.
  It is useful for the centres' effect on members, not for the whole problem."

**What lifts it**
- 1 → 2: say what the source lets you learn about the issue, with a quote.
- 2 → 3: ask what the question needs that this source does not give.
- 3 → 4: check it against another source, or against its purpose.

**Common slips:** answering reliability only and never saying "so it is / is not
useful for …"; listing what is missing with no link to the question.

## 5. Purpose

**The question:** "Why was Source G published?" · "Why did the cartoonist draw this?"

| Level | What the answer does |
|---|---|
| 1 | Describes the source or the event around it. |
| 2 | Gives the **message**: what the source wants to say, supported. |
| 3 | Gives the purpose in part: the message and the **audience**, or the message and the **outcome** wanted. |
| 4 | The full purpose: message, audience, and the outcome wanted — what the audience should think or **do** — supported, and tied to the situation at the time. |

**Example source.** **G**, a poster by a cycling group, published the week a town
council was debating a ban on bicycles on footpaths. It shows a child cycling to
school beside a busy road with lorries. Caption: "Where do you want her to ride?"

**Question:** Why was Source G published?

- **Level 1:** "It shows a child cycling next to lorries."
- **Level 2:** "It says that cycling on the road is dangerous for children. The child
  is drawn small beside large lorries."
- **Level 3:** Level 2, and: "It is aimed at residents and parents — the caption asks
  'you' directly."
- **Level 4:** Level 3, and: "It was published in the week of the council's debate.
  The group wants residents to feel that a footpath ban would push children onto
  the road, so that they speak up against the ban before the council decides."

**What lifts it**
- 1 → 2: say what the source wants people to believe.
- 2 → 3: say who it is talking to.
- 3 → 4: say what it wants those people to do, and why at that moment.

**Common slips:** "to inform people" as the purpose; an outcome with no audience;
ignoring the date or the provenance line.

## 6. The "how far do you agree" question

**The question:** "'[A statement about the issue.]' Using the sources in this case
study, explain how far you would agree with this statement."

This is the last source question. It uses **all** the sources.

| Level | What the answer does |
|---|---|
| 1 | Answers from own opinion, or names sources without using what they say. |
| 2 | One side only: sources that agree **or** sources that disagree, each used with a quote and an explanation. |
| 3 | Both sides: at least one source that agrees and one that disagrees, each used properly. |
| 4 | Level 3, and a conclusion that **weighs** the sources — which side has the more trustworthy or more complete evidence, and why. Not a recount of both sides. |

"Used properly" = which source · the quote · how it supports or challenges the statement.

**Example.** Statement: "The new mall is good for the neighbourhood."
(Sources D and E from scheme 3, plus **H**, a shop owner: "My sundry shop has lost
half its customers since the mall's supermarket opened.")

- **Level 1:** "I agree because malls are convenient."
- **Level 2:** "I agree. Source D says the mall 'will bring jobs and convenience to
  everyone', so residents gain."
- **Level 3:** Level 2, and: "Source E disagrees: '62% of households' are worried
  about traffic and noise. Source H also disagrees — the shop owner 'lost half' his
  customers."
- **Level 4:** Level 3, and: "The sources against the statement are stronger. D comes
  from the developer, who gains from saying so. E counts actual households and H
  describes a loss that has already happened. So I agree only to a small extent:
  the mall brings convenience, but the people living and working beside it carry
  the cost."

**What lifts it**
- 1 → 2: use a source. Quote it, then say how it backs the statement.
- 2 → 3: find a source for the other side.
- 3 → 4: do not end with "so there are two sides". Say which sources you trust more, and why.

**Common slips:** grouping sources with no quotes ("A, B and C agree"); a source placed
on the wrong side; a conclusion that only repeats the body.

## 7. Structured response

An extract is given as a starting point only.

### 7(a) — "Explain two ways / two reasons …"

| Level | What the answer does |
|---|---|
| 1 | Lists or describes ways. No explanation of how they work. |
| 2 | Explains **one** way: the point, an example, and how it leads to the result in the question. |
| 3 | Explains **two** ways, each to that standard. |

**Example question:** Explain two ways citizens can help to manage the challenges of an ageing population.

- **Level 1:** "Citizens can volunteer and save money."
- **Level 2:** "Citizens can volunteer at senior activity centres. Befriending visits
  mean seniors who live alone have someone checking on them, so fewer need paid
  care early. This eases the load on public services."
- **Level 3:** Level 2, and a second way to the same standard — for example saving
  through CPF so that fewer seniors depend on public funds.

### 7(b) — "Do you agree that X is more important than Y?"

| Level | What the answer does |
|---|---|
| 1 | Describes X or Y. No link to the question. |
| 2 | Explains **one** factor: the point, an example, and how it leads to the result. |
| 3 | Explains **both** factors to that standard. |
| 4 | Level 3, and a conclusion that **weighs** them on a stated basis — which matters more, and under what condition. |

**Example question:** "Government action matters more than individual effort in
keeping Singapore's society harmonious." Do you agree?

- **Level 4 conclusion, as a model:** "Government action sets the limits — laws and
  shared spaces make sure groups meet and no group is attacked. But laws cannot make
  neighbours trust one another; that comes from what individuals do every day.
  So government action matters more for preventing conflict, and individual effort
  matters more for building real ties. Without the first, the second has no safe
  ground to start from, so I agree to a large extent."

**What lifts it (both parts)**
- 1 → 2: after the point and example, add "this leads to … because …".
- 2 → 3: do the same for the second way or the other factor.
- 3 → 4 (part b only): pick a basis for comparing — which comes first, which lasts
  longer, which reaches more people — and judge on it.

**Common slips:** two points that are the same idea; an example with no link back to
the question; a conclusion that says "both are important" and stops.
