## Part 1 — Situational writing (Paper 1 Section B, 30 marks, 250–350 words)

### What earns the marks

Task Fulfilment is 10 marks, Language is 20. Task Fulfilment looks at three things:

1. **Every required point is answered and developed.** Top band = all points, in detail.
2. **Purpose, audience and context are clear.** Who is reading, why you are writing, how formal.
3. **The given information is used.** The task comes with a visual text. Use what it says.

Layout is not a marking line of its own. A wrong layout costs marks only because it shows
the writer missed the audience or the context.

### Before writing — four questions

- **Who** am I, and **who** reads this?
- **Why** am I writing? Put this in the first paragraph.
- **What** are the points the task asks for? Number them on the question paper.
- **What text type** does the task name? Use the task's own word.
  "Write a proposal" is a proposal. "Write a letter to propose" is a letter.

### One checklist per text type

**Formal letter**
- Your address, then the date in full (6 October 2026).
- The reader's name or title, and address.
- Greeting and close go in pairs:
  - "Dear Sir or Madam," → "Yours faithfully,"
  - "Dear Mr Tan," → "Yours sincerely,"
- A short subject line saying what the letter is about.
- Paragraph 1: who you are and why you are writing.
- One required point per paragraph, each developed.
- Last paragraph: what you want to happen next.
- Your full name under the close.

**Informal letter**
- Your address and the date.
- "Dear Wei Ling," (first name).
- A warm first line, then the reason for writing.
- The required points, still one per paragraph.
- A friendly close ("Best wishes," / "See you soon,") and your first name only.
- Friendly is not careless: full sentences, no text-message spelling.

**E-mail**
- To, From, Subject. The subject says the purpose in a few words.
- Formal e-mail: greeting and close as in a formal letter. No addresses.
- Informal e-mail: as an informal letter. No addresses.
- Your name at the end; add your role if the reader does not know you.

**Speech or talk**
- Greet the audience by who they are ("Good morning, Mr Lim, teachers and fellow students.").
- Say who you are and what you will talk about.
- Speak to the audience: "you", "we", a question now and then.
- One required point per section, with a clear move from one to the next.
- End with what you want them to do, then thank them.
- No address, no "Yours sincerely".

**Report**
- To, From, Date, and a title that says what the report is about.
- Paragraph 1: why the report is written.
- Facts in order (what happened, when, where, who), or one heading per required point.
- Neutral tone. No chatty lines.
- End with a recommendation if the task asks for one.
- Your name and role at the end. No "Yours sincerely".

**Proposal**
- To, From, Date, and a title.
- What you propose and why it is needed.
- The details the task asks for (what, who, when, cost, benefits).
- Why this choice is the best one, using the given information.
- A polite closing line asking for approval. Your name and role.

**Article**
- A title. "Written by" and your name under it, or at the end.
- An opening that tells the reader why this matters to them.
- One required point per paragraph.
- Written for the readers the task names (schoolmates, the public).
- A closing thought. No greeting, no close.

### Feedback lines the marker can use

- "The task asks for three things. You answered two. The missing one is …"
- "You are writing to your principal. 'Hey' and 'gonna' are too casual here."
- "Paragraph 1 does not say why you are writing. Say it in your first two sentences."
- "The poster gives the date and the cost. Use them — they are there to be used."
- "You greeted 'Dear Sir or Madam', so the close is 'Yours faithfully'."
- "This is a speech. Speak to the audience: use 'you' and 'we'."
- "About 480 words. The task asks for 250–350. Cut the second example."

### House style (school habits, not SEAB rules — Adrian picks)

The draft above uses: a comma after the greeting and the close · a plain subject line ·
a blank line between paragraphs · "Yours sincerely" on a formal e-mail.
A student who follows their own school's habit is **not** marked down.

## Part 2 — Reading the essay question (Paper 1 Section C, 30 marks, 350–500 words)

Content is 10 marks ("addressing the task"), Language is 20. Top band for Content = every
part of the task addressed and developed in detail. So the first job is to read the question.

### Step 1 — What kind of question is it?

| The question looks like | It wants |
|---|---|
| "Write about a time when …" | A story or a personal account, with what you felt and learnt |
| "Describe …" | A place, person or event made vivid, with your thoughts on it |
| "… Do you agree?" / "How far do you agree?" | Your stand, argued, with the other side answered |
| "What are your views?" / "What do you think about …?" | The different sides weighed, then your view |
| A statement with "only / never / always / all / no" | See Step 2 |
| "What more can be done …?" / "… enough?" / "… better?" | See Step 3 |

### Step 2 — Is there an absolute word?

Words like **only, never, always, all, every, no, the key, the most**.

- Circle it. The essay must deal with that word, not just the topic.
- Ask: why would someone believe this? Give that view its due.
- Then test it: is there an exception? Has it changed over time? Who does it not fit?
- Say plainly how far you agree.

Trap: writing about the topic and ignoring the word.
"Education is the only way to …" is not answered by an essay on why education is good.
It is answered by asking whether anything else also works.

### Step 3 — Is there a comparing word?

Words like **more, better, enough, still, further**.

"What more can be done?" takes for granted that something is already done and it falls short.
For each paragraph:

1. **Now** — what is already done.
2. **Gap** — why that is not enough.
3. **More** — what should be added, and why it would work.

Trap: listing what is done now and stopping there.

### Step 4 — Plan before writing

- Define the key words in the question in your own words.
- Write your stand in one sentence.
- Three reasons. Each needs one real example.
- One point from the other side, and your answer to it.
- An ending that answers the question — not a repeat of the introduction.

### Feedback lines the marker can use

- "The question says 'only'. Your essay never deals with that word."
- "The question asks what *more* can be done. You listed what is done now."
- "Your stand changes: the introduction says ban it, the ending says limit it."
- "Paragraph 3 makes a claim with no example. Add one you know to be true."
- "You did not answer the other side. One paragraph on it would lift the content."
- "This reads as a story, but the question asks for your views."

Never suggested: made-up statistics, memorised openings, big words for their own sake.

## Part 3 — Oral (Paper 4, 30 marks)

- **Planned Response, 15 marks.** A video clip and a prompt on a screen. Ten minutes to
  prepare. Speak for up to two minutes.
- **Spoken Interaction, 15 marks.** A discussion with the examiners on a topic linked to the
  clip. They do not ask what people said in the clip.
- There is no reading aloud.

### Planned Response — a two-minute shape

1. Answer the prompt in your first sentence.
2. Two or three reasons. Tie at least one to what you saw in the clip.
3. One example from your own life.
4. Close by saying your view again in one line.

In the ten minutes: note key words only. Do not write sentences to read out.

### Spoken Interaction — the kinds of question and a frame for each

| The examiner asks | A good answer |
|---|---|
| About you ("Would you like to …?", "Have you ever …?") | Answer → why → a short true story → what you took from it |
| For your opinion ("Do you think …?", "How far do you agree …?") | Your view → a reason → an example → the other side in a line → your view again |
| For ideas ("What can schools do to …?") | Two or three ideas, each with why it would work → which one is best |
| "What if …?" | What you would do → why → what could go wrong |

The basic move in every answer: **view → reason → example → wider point**.

### Six weak patterns to name in feedback

1. Not answering the question that was asked.
2. Describing instead of giving a view.
3. One-line answers with no reason.
4. Going on with no end point.
5. Answers that could be about anything — no real example.
6. Sounding memorised: essay words, no pauses, the same answer for any question.

Speak the way you would to a teacher you respect: clear, full sentences, plain words.

### Themes that come up often

School life and learning · family and friends · technology · the environment · health and
sport · community and volunteering · older people · Singapore's places and food.

Practise thinking of one true example from your own life for each.
