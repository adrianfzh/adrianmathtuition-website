Likely Q1 · AM · Trigonometry (Identities) · 3 marks
Why it might be Advanced: The student must spot that cos^6 x + sin^6 x is a sum of cubes in cos^2 x and sin^2 x and use a^3+b^3=(a+b)^3-3ab(a+b), an algebra idea not usually drilled for trig identities; the double-angle steps after it are routine. (hardest part )
Prove the identity $\frac{1}{\sec^6 x}+\frac{1}{\csc^6 x}\equiv\frac{5+3\cos 4x}{8}$.
Worked solution
Start from the LHS, the messier side, and turn the reciprocals into sine and cosine.
LHS $=\cos^6 x+\sin^6 x$
Treat this as a sum of cubes in $\cos^2 x$ and $\sin^2 x$: $a^3+b^3=(a+b)^3-3ab(a+b)$.
$=\left(\cos^2 x+\sin^2 x\right)^3-3\sin^2 x\cos^2 x\left(\cos^2 x+\sin^2 x\right)$
Use $\sin^2 x+\cos^2 x=1$.
$=1-3\sin^2 x\cos^2 x$
Use $\sin 2x=2\sin x\cos x$, so $\sin^2 x\cos^2 x=\frac{1}{4}\sin^2 2x$.
$=1-\frac{3}{4}\sin^2 2x$
The target has $\cos 4x$, so pick the form $\cos 4x=1-2\sin^2 2x$, which gives $\sin^2 2x=\frac{1-\cos 4x}{2}$.
$=1-\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{1-\cos 4x}{2}$
$=1-\frac{3}{8}+\frac{3}{8}\cos 4x$
$=\frac{5+3\cos 4x}{8}=$ RHS (Shown)

Likely Q2 · AM · Trigonometry (Identities), Trigonometry (Equations), Trigonometry (Ratios) · 8 marks
Why it might be Advanced: With no signpost, the student must chain sin 50° = cos 40° and cos 70° = sin 20°, then spot that 40° = 2 × 20°, the same undrilled angle-link idea as the kept 130° → 50° → 25° question; (b) is a routine combine-fractions proof plus a zero-denominator check. (hardest part (a))
(a) It is given that $\sin 50^\circ = m$. Without using a calculator, express $\cos 70^\circ$ in terms of $m$. [2]
Answer: $\cos 70^\circ = \sqrt{\dfrac{1-m}{2}}$
(b) [6]
(i) Prove that $\dfrac{\cos x}{\cos x - \sin x} + \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x + \sin x} = 1 + \tan 2x$. [4]
Answer: Shown
(ii) Find the values of $x$, where $0 < x < \pi$, for which the identity in part (i) is undefined. Leave your answers in terms of $\pi$. [2]
Answer: $x = \dfrac{\pi}{4},\ \dfrac{3\pi}{4}$
Worked solution
(a) First link the two angles. $\sin 50^\circ = \cos(90^\circ - 50^\circ) = \cos 40^\circ$, so $\cos 40^\circ = m$.
Also $\cos 70^\circ = \sin(90^\circ - 70^\circ) = \sin 20^\circ$.
Now $40^\circ = 2 \times 20^\circ$, and we want a sine at the end, so pick the sine form of $\cos 2A$: $\cos 40^\circ = 1 - 2\sin^2 20^\circ$.
$m = 1 - 2\sin^2 20^\circ$
$\sin^2 20^\circ = \dfrac{1-m}{2}$
$20^\circ$ is acute, so $\sin 20^\circ > 0$ and we take the positive root.
$\cos 70^\circ = \sin 20^\circ = \sqrt{\dfrac{1-m}{2}}$

(b)(i) Work on the LHS only and combine into a single fraction.
LHS $= \dfrac{\cos x}{\cos x - \sin x} + \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x + \sin x}$
$= \dfrac{\cos x(\cos x + \sin x) + \sin x(\cos x - \sin x)}{(\cos x - \sin x)(\cos x + \sin x)}$
$= \dfrac{\cos^2 x + \sin x\cos x + \sin x\cos x - \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x - \sin^2 x}$
$= \dfrac{(\cos^2 x - \sin^2 x) + 2\sin x\cos x}{\cos^2 x - \sin^2 x}$
Use $\cos 2x = \cos^2 x - \sin^2 x$ and $\sin 2x = 2\sin x\cos x$:
$= \dfrac{\cos 2x + \sin 2x}{\cos 2x}$
$= 1 + \dfrac{\sin 2x}{\cos 2x}$
$= 1 + \tan 2x =$ RHS (shown)

(ii) The identity fails wherever a denominator is zero.
$\cos x - \sin x = 0 \Rightarrow \tan x = 1$; $\cos x + \sin x = 0 \Rightarrow \tan x = -1$.
(These are exactly where $\cos 2x = (\cos x - \sin x)(\cos x + \sin x) = 0$, so $\tan 2x$ is also undefined there — no new values.)
Basic angle $\alpha = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$.
$\tan x = 1$ (1st quadrant in range): $x = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$
$\tan x = -1$ (2nd quadrant in range): $x = \pi - \dfrac{\pi}{4} = \dfrac{3\pi}{4}$
$x = \dfrac{\pi}{4},\ \dfrac{3\pi}{4}$

Likely Q3 · EM · Algebra (Simultaneous Equations), Numbers (Ratio) · 3 marks
Why it might be Advanced: Nothing says how many of each token were won, so the student must realise that 3a + 7b = 13 needs whole-number solutions and find a = 2, b = 1 by trying cases. This integer reasoning is not drilled in E Math simultaneous equations; the ratio set-up after it is routine. (hardest part )
At a games arcade, Priya collects only blue tokens and red tokens. Each blue token is worth $3$ points and each red token is worth $7$ points. At first, the ratio of the number of blue tokens to the number of red tokens she has is $5:4$. She then wins some more tokens with a total value of $13$ points. The ratio of the number of blue tokens to the number of red tokens she has becomes $4:3$. Find the number of blue tokens Priya had at first.
Worked solution
Let $a$ blue and $b$ red tokens be won, so $3a+7b=13$ with $a$, $b$ whole numbers.
If $b=0$: $3a=13$, not a whole number.
If $b=1$: $3a=6$, so $a=2$.
If $b\ge 2$: $7b\ge 14>13$, impossible.
So she wins $2$ blue tokens and $1$ red token.
Let $x$ = number of blue tokens and $y$ = number of red tokens at first.
Before: $\frac{x}{y}=\frac{5}{4}\Rightarrow 4x=5y$ ... (1)
After: $\frac{x+2}{y+1}=\frac{4}{3}\Rightarrow 3x+6=4y+4\Rightarrow 3x-4y=-2$ ... (2)
From (1): $x=\frac{5y}{4}$.
Substitute into (2): $\frac{15y}{4}-4y=-2$
$-\frac{y}{4}=-2$
$y=8$
$x=\frac{5(8)}{4}=10$
Check: $10:8=5:4$ and $12:9=4:3$. ✓
Number of blue tokens at first $=10$.

Maybe Q4 · AM · Circles, Coordinate Geometry · 9 marks
Why it might be Advanced: The point R moves with t, so the student must write CR² as a quadratic in t and complete the square to show it is always above 25. Testing inside or outside the circle is drilled, but proving it for every real t is less so; borderline. (hardest part (c))
The lines $y=x+3$ and $x+2y=0$ intersect at the point $C$. A circle, with centre $C$, passes through the point $Q(1,\,5)$.
(a) Show that the equation of the circle is $x^2+y^2+4x-2y-20=0$. [3]
Answer: Shown: centre $C(-2,\,1)$, radius $5$, so $(x+2)^2+(y-1)^2=25$, i.e. $x^2+y^2+4x-2y-20=0$.
(b) Find the equation of the tangent to the circle at $Q$. Give your answer in the form $px+qy=r$, where $p$, $q$ and $r$ are integers. [3]
Answer: $3x+4y=23$
(c) The point $R$ has coordinates $(3-t,\;t+4)$, where $t$ can be any real number. Determine whether $R$ lies inside the circle, on the circle or outside the circle. Show your working clearly. [3]
Answer: $R$ lies outside the circle for every real value of $t$, since $CR^2=2(t-1)^2+32\ge 32>25$.
Worked solution
Sketch first: mark the centre where the two lines cross, the point $Q$, and the circle through $Q$.
(a) Substitute $y=x+3$ into $x+2y=0$: $x+2(x+3)=0$
$3x+6=0 \Rightarrow x=-2$, so $y=-2+3=1$. Centre $C(-2,\,1)$.
Radius $=CQ=\sqrt{(1-(-2))^2+(5-1)^2}=\sqrt{9+16}=5$
Equation: $(x+2)^2+(y-1)^2=25$
$x^2+4x+4+y^2-2y+1=25$
$x^2+y^2+4x-2y-20=0$ (shown)
(b) Gradient of radius $CQ=\frac{5-1}{1-(-2)}=\frac{4}{3}$
Tangent is perpendicular to the radius at $Q$, so gradient of tangent $=-\frac{3}{4}$
$y-5=-\frac{3}{4}(x-1)$
$4y-20=-3x+3$
$3x+4y=23$
Check with $Q$: $3(1)+4(5)=23$ ✓
(c) Compare $CR^2$ with $r^2=25$.
$CR^2=\big((3-t)-(-2)\big)^2+\big((t+4)-1\big)^2=(5-t)^2+(t+3)^2$
$=25-10t+t^2+t^2+6t+9=2t^2-4t+34$
Complete the square: $2t^2-4t+34=2(t^2-2t)+34=2(t-1)^2-2+34=2(t-1)^2+32$
Since $(t-1)^2\ge 0$ for all real $t$, $CR^2\ge 32>25=r^2$
So $CR>r$ for every real $t$: $R$ always lies outside the circle.
(Equivalently, substituting $R$ into the LHS of the circle equation gives $2(t-1)^2+7>0$.)

Maybe Q7 · AM · Differentiation (Increasing and Decreasing Functions), Differentiation (Maximum and Minimum) · 5 marks
Why it might be Advanced: The student is given the graph of h' rather than h, and may wrongly take the turning points of h' (x = 2, 5.5) as stationary points of h or miss that touching the axis at x = 4 gives an inflexion. This is borderline because a sign-of-derivative table is routinely drilled. (hardest part (b))
A function $h$ is defined for $0 < x < 7$. The sketch shows the curve $y = h'(x)$, where $h'(x)$ is the derivative of $h(x)$. The curve meets the $x$-axis at exactly three points: it crosses the axis at $x = 1$ and at $x = 6$, and it touches the axis at $x = 4$. The curve has turning points at $x = 2$, $x = 4$ and $x = 5.5$.
(a) Using the sketch, find the range(s) of values of $x$ for which $h(x)$ is an increasing function. Justify your answer. [2]
Answer: $1 < x < 4$ and $4 < x < 6$ (accept $1 < x < 6$), since $h'(x) > 0$ there
(b) Find the $x$-coordinate of each stationary point on the curve $y = h(x)$. Determine the nature of each stationary point, explaining your reasoning. [3]
Answer: $x = 1$: minimum point; $x = 4$: point of inflexion; $x = 6$: maximum point
Worked solution
(a)
$h(x)$ is increasing where $h'(x) > 0$, i.e. where the curve $y = h'(x)$ lies above the $x$-axis.
For $0 < x < 1$ the curve is below the axis, so $h'(x) < 0$.
For $1 < x < 4$ the curve is above the axis, so $h'(x) > 0$.
At $x = 4$ the curve only touches the axis, so $h'(4) = 0$.
For $4 < x < 6$ the curve is above the axis again, so $h'(x) > 0$.
For $6 < x < 7$ the curve is below the axis, so $h'(x) < 0$.
Hence $h(x)$ is increasing for $1 < x < 4$ and $4 < x < 6$, because $h'(x) > 0$ there.
The turning points of $h'$ at $x = 2$ and $x = 5.5$ do not matter here. They tell you where the gradient of $h$ is largest, not where $h$ itself turns.

(b)
Stationary points of $h$ occur where $h'(x) = 0$, i.e. where the curve meets the $x$-axis: $x = 1$, $x = 4$, $x = 6$.
Read the sign of $h'(x)$ just before and just after each one, in increasing $x$:
$x = 1$: $h'(x) < 0$ just before, $0$ at $x=1$, $h'(x) > 0$ just after. The slope goes $\searrow\ \text{—}\ \nearrow$, so $x = 1$ is a minimum point.
$x = 4$: $h'(x) > 0$ just before, $0$ at $x=4$, $h'(x) > 0$ just after. The slope goes $\nearrow\ \text{—}\ \nearrow$ with no change of sign, so $x = 4$ is a point of inflexion.
$x = 6$: $h'(x) > 0$ just before, $0$ at $x=6$, $h'(x) < 0$ just after. The slope goes $\nearrow\ \text{—}\ \searrow$, so $x = 6$ is a maximum point.
Answer: minimum point at $x = 1$, point of inflexion at $x = 4$, maximum point at $x = 6$.

Water flows into and out of a reservoir. The volume of water in the reservoir, $V$ m$^3$, $t$ hours after midnight is modelled for $0 < t < 12$. The diagram shows the graph of $\frac{dV}{dt}$ against $t$. The graph cuts the $t$-axis at $t = 2$, $t = 6$ and $t = 10$. It has a minimum point at $(4, -3)$ and a maximum point at $(8, 2.5)$.
(a) Using the graph, find the intervals of time during which the volume of water in the reservoir is decreasing. Give a reason for your answer. [2]

(b) Find the value(s) of $t$ at which $V$ has a maximum value. Justify your answer. [3]

Worked solution
(a) $V$ is decreasing when $\frac{dV}{dt} < 0$, i.e. where the graph lies below the $t$-axis.
From the graph, the curve is below the $t$-axis between $t = 2$ and $t = 6$, and from $t = 10$ to the end of the domain.
So $V$ is decreasing for $2 < t < 6$ and $10 < t < 12$.

(b) $V$ is maximised, so set the derivative of $V$ to zero: $\frac{dV}{dt} = 0$.

At $t = 10$: just before, $\frac{dV}{dt} > 0$; just after, $\frac{dV}{dt} < 0$. So $+ \to -$, which gives a maximum.
Therefore $V$ has a maximum value at $t = 2$ and at $t = 10$.
