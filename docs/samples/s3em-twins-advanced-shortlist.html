Maybe Q1 · Algebra (Graph on Graph Paper) · 3 marks
Why it might be Advanced: sketch 1/x² with parabola to count solutions of a combined equation (hardest part whole)
(a) Using one set of axes, sketch the following two graphs, showing where each graph meets the axes, if it does. [0]
(i) $y = 4 - x^2$ [1]
Answer: A parabola opening downwards (∩-shaped), symmetric about the $y$-axis, with maximum point $(0, 4)$, cutting the $x$-axis at $(-2, 0)$ and $(2, 0)$.
(ii) $y = \frac{1}{x^2}$ [1]
Answer: Two branches lying above the $x$-axis in the 1st and 2nd quadrants, symmetric about the $y$-axis; the $x$-axis and $y$-axis are asymptotes and the graph does not meet either axis.
(b) Use your sketches to write down how many solutions the equation $4 - x^2 - \frac{1}{x^2} = 0$ has. [1]
Answer: $4$
Worked solution
(a)(i) $y = 4 - x^2$ is a quadratic with a negative $x^2$ term, so it opens downwards.
When $x = 0$, $y = 4$, so the maximum point is $(0, 4)$.
When $y = 0$, $x^2 = 4$, so $x = -2$ or $x = 2$.
Sketch a ∩-shaped curve through $(-2, 0)$, $(0, 4)$ and $(2, 0)$, symmetric about the $y$-axis.
(a)(ii) $y = \frac{1}{x^2}$: $x^2 > 0$ for every $x \neq 0$, so $y > 0$ always.
The graph never touches either axis: $x = 0$ is not allowed and $y$ is never $0$.
As $x \to 0$, $y$ becomes very large; as $x$ gets large (positive or negative), $y \to 0$.
Sketch two branches, one in the 1st quadrant and one in the 2nd quadrant, mirror images in the $y$-axis.
(b) Rewrite the equation: $4 - x^2 - \frac{1}{x^2} = 0 \Rightarrow 4 - x^2 = \frac{1}{x^2}$.
So the solutions are the $x$-coordinates where the two graphs cross.
For $x > 0$: near $x = 0$, $\frac{1}{x^2}$ is above $4$, so it is above the parabola; at $x = 1$, $\frac{1}{x^2} = 1 < 3 = 4 - x^2$. They cross once between $0$ and $1$.
At $x = 2$, the parabola is at $0$ but $\frac{1}{x^2} = \frac{1}{4} > 0$. They cross again between $1$ and $2$.
Beyond that the parabola is negative and $\frac{1}{x^2}$ stays positive, so no more crossings.
Both graphs are symmetric about the $y$-axis, so there are another $2$ crossings for $x < 0$.
Total: $2 + 2$ crossings.
**Answer:** $4$ solutions

Maybe Q2 · Numbers (Prime Factorization) · 4 marks
Why it might be Advanced: prime q must divide out a lone prime factor so quotient is a cube (hardest part (ii))
(i) Write 1188 as the product of its prime factors, giving your answer in index notation. [1]
Answer: $1188 = 2^2 \times 3^3 \times 11$
(ii) The positive integer $n$ satisfies $\frac{1188p}{q} = n^3$, where $p$ and $q$ are prime numbers. Find $p$, $q$ and $n$. [3]
Answer: $p = 2$, $q = 11$, $n = 6$
Worked solution
(i)
$1188 = 2 \times 594$
$= 2 \times 2 \times 297$
$= 2^2 \times 3 \times 99$
$= 2^2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 33$
$= 2^2 \times 3^3 \times 11$
**Answer:** $1188 = 2^2 \times 3^3 \times 11$

(ii)
$\frac{1188p}{q} = \frac{2^2 \times 3^3 \times 11 \times p}{q}$
For a perfect cube, every index must be a multiple of 3.
$3^3$ is already a cube.
$2^2$ needs one more 2, so multiply by 2: $p = 2$.
$11^1$ cannot be raised to $11^3$ with one prime, so divide it out: $q = 11$.
$\frac{1188 \times 2}{11} = 2^3 \times 3^3 = 216$
$n^3 = 216$
$n = 2 \times 3 = 6$
**Answer:** $p = 2$, $q = 11$, $n = 6$
Your call:
Maybe Q3 · Numbers (Prime Factorization) · 3 marks
Why it might be Advanced: p and q prime so cube needs q dividing out 11, p supplying missing 5 (hardest part (b))
A toy company packs identical small wooden cubes into boxes. Each box holds exactly 2200 cubes.
(a) Write 2200 as a product of prime factors, giving your answer in index notation. [1]
Answer: $2200 = 2^3 \times 5^2 \times 11$
(b) A teacher opens $p$ full boxes and shares all the cubes equally among $q$ groups of students, with no cubes left over. Each group then uses every one of its cubes to build one large solid cube. Given that $p$ and $q$ are both prime numbers, find $p$ and $q$. [2]
Answer: $p = 5$, $q = 11$
Worked solution
**(a)**
Divide by the smallest prime each time.
$2200 = 2 \times 1100$
$= 2 \times 2 \times 550$
$= 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 275$
$= 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 5 \times 55$
$= 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 5 \times 5 \times 11$
**Answer:** $2200 = 2^3 \times 5^2 \times 11$

**(b)**
Number of small cubes each group gets $= \dfrac{2200p}{q}$.
A large solid cube uses a perfect cube number of small cubes.
So $\dfrac{2200p}{q} = \dfrac{2^3 \times 5^2 \times 11 \times p}{q}$ is a perfect cube.
For a perfect cube, every index must be a multiple of 3.
The index of 2 is 3. This is already a multiple of 3.
The index of 5 is 2. Multiply by one more 5 to make it 3, so $p = 5$.
The index of 11 is 1. Divide by 11 to remove it, so $q = 11$.
Check: $\dfrac{2200 \times 5}{11} = 2^3 \times 5^3 = 1000 = 10^3$.
**Answer:** $p = 5$, $q = 11$

Maybe Q4 · Statistics · 3 marks
Why it might be Advanced: mean cannot fall by 0.5: impossible negative score, needs inequality reasoning (hardest part whole)
At an archery club's open day, each of 15 members shot 10 arrows at a target. The dot diagram shows the number of arrows that hit the target for each member.

The coach, Mr Rahim, also shot 10 arrows, but his result is not shown in the diagram. He joked that he shot so badly that including his result would lower the mean number of hits by at least 0.5.

Could Mr Rahim's statement be true? Justify your answer, showing your working clearly.
Worked solution
Total number of hits for the 15 members
$= 3 + (5 \times 2) + (6 \times 2) + (7 \times 4) + (8 \times 3) + (9 \times 2) + 10$
$= 105$
Mean for the 15 members $= \dfrac{105}{15} = 7$
For the mean to fall by at least 0.5, the new mean must be at most $7 - 0.5 = 6.5$.
With Mr Rahim there are 16 results, so the total must be at most $6.5 \times 16 = 104$.
Mr Rahim's number of hits must be at most $104 - 105 = -1$.
The number of hits cannot be less than 0, so this is impossible.
**Answer:** No, his statement cannot be true.
Your call:
Maybe Q5 · Statistics · 3 marks
Why it might be Advanced: mode forces one unknown to be 15; non-unique pair reasoning (hardest part (a))
Nine swimmers each recorded the number of laps they completed during a training session.
Their results are listed below, where $p$ and $q$ are whole numbers.
$$\begin{array}{ccccccccc} 9 & 15 & 7 & p & 11 & 15 & 9 & q & 14 \end{array}$$
(a) The mean number of laps is 12 and the mode is 15.
Find a possible pair of values of $p$ and $q$. [2]
Answer: $p = 15$, $q = 13$ (or $p = 13$, $q = 15$)
(b) Hence find the median number of laps completed. [1]
Answer: $13$
Worked solution
**(a)**
Use the mean to find the total first.
Total of the 9 values $= 9 \times 12 = 108$
Sum of the 7 known values $= 9 + 15 + 7 + 11 + 15 + 9 + 14 = 80$
$p + q = 108 - 80 = 28$
Now use the mode.
So far 15 appears twice and 9 appears twice.
For 15 to be the only mode, it must appear more often than every other value.
So one of the unknowns is 15.
Let $p = 15$.
$q = 28 - 15 = 13$
Check: 15 appears three times and 9 appears twice, so the mode is 15.
(If $p = q = 14$, then 14 would appear three times and be the mode, so this is rejected.)
**Answer:** $p = 15$, $q = 13$ (or $p = 13$, $q = 15$)

**(b)**
Arrange the 9 values in ascending order.
$7,\ 9,\ 9,\ 11,\ 13,\ 14,\ 15,\ 15,\ 15$
Median position $= \frac{9 + 1}{2} = 5$th value
The 5th value is 13.
**Answer:** $13$
