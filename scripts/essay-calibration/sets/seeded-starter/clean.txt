I had always thought that Mr Tan, the caretaker of our block, disliked children. He never smiled when we passed him at the lift lobby, and he once shouted at my brother for kicking a ball against the wall. For years I avoided him whenever I could.

One evening in December, the rain was so heavy that the drains along our street overflowed. I was walking home from the library when I saw a small brown dog trapped on the far side of the canal. The water was rising quickly, and the dog was shivering against the railing. I wanted to help, but I was afraid of the current, so I stood there and did nothing.

Then Mr Tan appeared. He was carrying a long wooden plank from the storeroom, and without saying a word he laid it across the narrowest part of the canal. He crossed it slowly, picked up the dog and carried it back in his arms. When he reached me, his shirt was soaked and his hands were shaking. He looked at me and said quietly, "It belongs to the family on the fourth floor. Will you take it up to them?"

I nodded. As I held the dog, I noticed the scratches on his arms and the mud on his trousers. He had not hesitated for a second, while I had only watched. I felt ashamed of what I had believed about him.

The next morning, I knocked on the door of his small office to thank him. He seemed surprised, and then he laughed for the first time that I could remember. He told me that he shouted at children only when they played near the canal, because a boy had fallen into it many years ago. He had been the one who pulled him out.

Since that day, I have greeted Mr Tan every morning. I learnt that a stern face does not always mean a cold heart, and that I should be slower to judge the people around me.
