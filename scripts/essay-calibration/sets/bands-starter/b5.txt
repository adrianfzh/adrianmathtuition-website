For most of Secondary Two, I believed that kindness had to be loud to count. It was the kind of thing announced at assembly: a cheque presented on stage, a class that had collected the most canned food. I did not expect to change my mind because of a pencil case.

It happened on the morning of the mid-year Mathematics paper. I had left home late, run for the bus and arrived at the hall with my heart still hammering. Only when the invigilator told us to place our stationery on the desk did I unzip my bag and find that my pencil case was not in it. I could picture it exactly, lying on my bed beside the notes I had been revising. Around me, two hundred students laid out pens and calculators in neat rows. I had nothing, and the paper would begin in four minutes.

I was too embarrassed to raise my hand. I simply sat there, staring at the empty desk and feeling the panic rise like water in a blocked sink. Then something slid across the gap between my desk and the next. It was a transparent pencil case, already opened, holding two pens, a pencil, a ruler and an eraser cut neatly in half. The girl beside me, Farah, whom I had barely spoken to all year, did not even look up. She had kept one pen and her calculator for herself and pushed everything else towards me, as casually as if she were passing the salt.

There was no time to thank her. The paper began, and for two hours I wrote with a stranger's pen. What surprised me was how calm I felt. The questions were no easier than I had feared, yet my hands had stopped shaking, because somebody had noticed my trouble and quietly decided that it mattered.

Afterwards I tried to return the pencil case with a speech I had rehearsed during the last ten minutes of the paper. Farah shrugged. "You would have done the same," she said. I was not certain that I would have, and that uncertainty stayed with me far longer than the examination result did.

In the weeks that followed, I began to look for the small gaps that other people fall into: the classmate without a partner for a project, the new boy eating alone at the far end of the canteen. Filling those gaps cost me very little, usually no more than half an eraser, yet I saw each time how much it steadied the person on the other side.

Farah and I are close friends now, although she still insists that she did nothing special that morning. Perhaps that is the point. An act of kindness does not need an audience to make a difference; it only needs someone who is paying attention at the right moment. She was, and because of her, I have tried to be ever since.
