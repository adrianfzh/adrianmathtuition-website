Many adults complain that teenagers are always looking at a screen. I agree that some young people spend too much time on their phones, but I do not think the phone itself is the problem. What matters is what we do with it.

First, a phone can waste a great deal of time. Short videos are designed to keep us watching, and one video quickly becomes twenty. My cousin once told me that he had planned to revise for an hour and had spent the whole evening scrolling instead. When this happens every night, schoolwork suffers and sleep suffers too. Teachers have noticed that tired students make more careless mistakes in class.

However, the same phone is also a useful tool. Last year, my classmates and I used a shared document to prepare for a group project. We could work on it from our own homes, and nobody had to travel across the island. I also use my phone to look up words I do not know and to listen to the news in the morning. A student who has no phone today would find it harder to keep up with the class.

Some people argue that schools should ban phones completely. I disagree with this view. A ban may work during lessons, but it does not teach us how to control ourselves. Sooner or later, every student will own a phone without any rules attached to it. It is better to learn good habits now, while parents and teachers can still guide us.

What, then, should young people do? I believe we should set limits that we choose ourselves. For example, I keep my phone in the living room after ten o'clock at night. This simple rule has helped me to fall asleep earlier. My friends have tried similar rules, and most of them say that they feel less anxious.

In conclusion, young people do spend many hours on their phones, and some of those hours are wasted. However, the answer is not to take the phone away. The answer is to teach every student to use it with care. If we manage that, the phone will remain a servant and never become a master.
